# Public-domain sources of explanatory writing as candidate training corpora

Research date: 2026-11-13. US copyright law only. No corpus was crawled or downloaded; only policy pages, robots.txt files and a handful of sample pages were read.

Verification legend used below: **[P]** = primary page read this session; **[S]** = search-result snippet or secondary source only (page not opened); **[BK]** = researcher's background knowledge, not verified this session (always placed under Inferences or Gaps).

Pages that refused automated fetch (HTTP 403 / Cloudflare block) and are therefore unverified: nih.gov web policies, newsinhealth.nih.gov, si.edu/termsofuse, feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/info, congress.gov/crs-products/about.

## 1. Are these US government sources actually public domain? (statements, exceptions, contractor flags)

### Takeaway
The statute and most agency reuse pages confirm that employee-written text is free of US copyright, but almost every agency carves out third-party, contractor and image content, and several named candidates are not public domain at all (FRED/St. Louis Fed, Smithsonian, Feynman Lectures) or are contractor-written (climate.gov, anything JPL/Caltech). CRS, GAO, BLS, EIA, USGS, NCI text, MedlinePlus health-topic summaries, Federal Reserve Board pages and VOA-exclusive text have clear, quotable public-domain statements.

### Cited Findings

**Statute and general rule**
- 17 U.S.C. 105(a): "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government"; the Government may still hold copyrights transferred to it. Section 101 defines the term as "a work prepared by an officer or employee of the United States Government" as part of that person's official duties. [P] — [copyright.gov, Title 17 ch. 1](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html)
- USA.gov: "Not everything that appears on a federal government website is a government work. Government work is something created by a U.S. government officer or employee as part of their official duties. Content on federal websites may include protected intellectual property used with the right holder's permission." [P] — [usa.gov/government-copyright](https://www.usa.gov/government-copyright)

**Agency statements (quoted)**
- **CRS**: "CRS reports, as works of the United States Government, are not subject to copyright protection in the United States"; a report may include third-party copyrighted material (images etc.) for which "you may need to obtain the permission of the copyright holder". [P] — [EveryCRSReport download page](https://www.everycrsreport.com/download.html)
- **GAO**: "GAO's products and information on GAO's Web site are not protected by copyright law in the United States" and "may be copied and distributed in their entirety without permission from GAO"; products "may contain copyrighted images or other material". [P] — [gao.gov/copyright](https://www.gao.gov/copyright)
- **BLS** (Beyond the Numbers, Occupational Outlook Handbook): "everything that we publish, both in hard copy and electronically, is in the public domain, except for previously copyrighted photographs and illustrations"; citation requested. [P] — [bls.gov linking and copyright](https://www.bls.gov/bls/linksite.htm)
- **EIA** (Energy Explained): "U.S. government publications are in the public domain and are not subject to copyright protection. You may use and/or distribute any of our data, files, databases, reports, graphs, charts, and other information products"; acknowledgment with publication date requested; photographs are under private licences and excluded. [P] — [eia.gov copyrights and reuse](https://www.eia.gov/about/copyrights_reuse.php)
- **USGS** (FAQs, Water Science School): "USGS-authored or produced data and information are considered to be in the U.S. Public Domain"; but "not all information, illustrations, or photographs on our site are", and non-USGS items are "generally marked as being copyrighted". [P] — [usgs.gov copyrights and credits](https://www.usgs.gov/information-policies-and-instructions/copyrights-and-credits)
- **NCI** (About Cancer): "Unless otherwise indicated, all text within National Cancer Institute (NCI) products is free of copyright and may be reused without our permission. Credit the National Cancer Institute as the source." Graphics are "a mix of copyrighted and copyright-free materials". The page does not address contractor-written text. [P] — [cancer.gov copyright and reuse](https://www.cancer.gov/policies/copyright-reuse)
- **MedlinePlus**: "Works produced by the federal government are not copyrighted under U.S. law" (covers health topic summaries, medical test information, recipes). NOT public domain: "Any content from the A.D.A.M. Medical Encyclopedia" and "Drug monographs from the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists"; RSS feeds are personal-use only. [P] — [MedlinePlus: using content](https://medlineplus.gov/about/using/usingcontent/)
- **CDC**: "Most of the information on the CDC and ATSDR websites is not subject to copyright, is in the public domain, and may be freely used or reproduced without obtaining copyright permission", but "some resources, as well as images ... are restricted in their use because they were developed by government contractors or grantees, or have been licensed by a third party." [P] — [cdc.gov agency materials](https://www.cdc.gov/other/agencymaterials.html)
- **Federal Reserve Board** (federalreserve.gov only): "Unless otherwise indicated, information on Board's website is in the public domain and may be copied and distributed without permission. Please cite to the Board as the source". [P] — [federalreserve.gov disclaimer](https://www.federalreserve.gov/disclaimer.htm)
- **VOA / VOA Learning English**: "All text, audio and video material produced exclusively by the Voice of America is in the public domain." Exceptions: licensed third-party material, and "All AP material is copyrighted and the property of Associated Press" and may not be stored "in a computer except for personal and non-commercial use". [P] — [VOA Learning English terms of use](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/p/6021.html)
- **NASA**: the media guidelines page says NASA images, audio and video "generally are not subject to copyright in the United States"; "NASA occasionally uses copyright-protected material of third parties with permission on its website", and such use "does not convey any rights to others". The page speaks about media, not explicitly about article text. [P] — [NASA images and media guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)

**Exceptions and non-public-domain candidates**
- **JPL/Caltech**: the JPL image-use policy covers images and video only; "Caltech makes no representations or warranties with respect to ownership of copyrights in the images". It sets no reuse terms for text. JPL is operated by Caltech, so JPL-authored text is not automatically a section 105 work. [P] — [JPL image use policy](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/jpl-image-use-policy/)
- **FRED / Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis**: "Everything you see on FRED belongs either to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis or a third party"; content is "the proprietary intellectual property of the Bank ... with all rights reserved"; prohibited uses include "any data mining, mirroring, robots, scraping, or similar data-gathering or extraction methods" and use "in connection with the development or training of any software program or system or machine learning". This is an explicit bar on the intended use. [P] — [FRED legal](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/legal/)
- **climate.gov (NOAA)**: content was produced by a contracted team of about 10 whose contracts ended 31 May 2025; the site stopped publishing new content on 1 July 2025; the archive remained online. Contractor authorship means section 105 status cannot be assumed for its articles. [S] — [NPR, 12 June 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/06/12/nx-s1-5431660/climate-us-government-website-changes)
- **Smithsonian**: content carrying "usage conditions apply" may be used only for "personal, educational, and other non-commercial uses consistent with the principles of fair use"; only items marked CC0 (Open Access) are free for any use. [S] — [Smithsonian terms of use](https://www.si.edu/termsofuse) (page returned 403 to the fetch tool; wording from search snippets and an [archived 2020 copy](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20201218102546mp_/http://www.si.edu/Termsofuse))
- **Feynman Lectures on Physics**: not public domain. Secondary sources report the online edition is free to read only, that the posting conveys no right to download any part of the book, and that a DMCA takedown was filed on Caltech's behalf in 2014 against a GitHub copy. [S] — [Techdirt, 6 March 2014](https://www.techdirt.com/2014/03/06/disappointing-dmca-being-used-to-make-feynman-lectures-physics-less-accessible/). The site's own robots.txt, read this session, carries `Content-Signal: search=yes,ai-train=no,use=reference` and blocks training crawlers (ClaudeBot, CCBot, Google-Extended, Bytespider, Amazonbot, others). [P] — [feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/robots.txt](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/robots.txt)
- **CIA World Factbook**: the CIA ended publication on 4 February 2026 after more than six decades; reports differ on whether pages remain reachable; third-party archives exist. [S] — [AP via WTOP, Feb 2026](https://wtop.com/news/2026/02/cia-ends-publication-of-its-popular-world-factbook-reference-tool); [Data Rescue Project](https://www.datarescueproject.org/guest-post-the-world-factbook-is-gone/)

### Inferences
- The safest text is that with (a) an explicit agency statement covering text, and (b) staff rather than contractor authorship: CRS, GAO, BLS, EIA, USGS, Federal Reserve Board, MedlinePlus health-topic summaries.
- NASA Earth Observatory, NASA Science pages and NCI pages are very probably public-domain text, but NASA's public statement is framed around media and NASA web writing is frequently done by contractor staff; the NASA page does not resolve this.
- VOA Learning English news items are often adaptations of wire reports; the public-domain statement covers only material "produced exclusively by" VOA, so items crediting AP or Reuters as a source need to be filtered out. (Sourcing-credit practice is [BK]; the exclusion rule is [P] above.)
- Federal Reserve Bank content (FRED blog, St. Louis Fed education pages) should be dropped: it is both copyrighted and expressly closed to ML training. Federal Reserve Board (federalreserve.gov) content is fine.

### Gaps
- NIH general copyright statement, NIH News in Health reprint policy, NIH Research Matters and NIGMS Biomedical Beat: nih.gov and newsinhealth.nih.gov returned 403 / Cloudflare block. [BK]: NIH News in Health has historically invited reprinting and its text is generally treated as public domain; not verified this session.
- NOAA (SciJinks, Ocean Service) copyright statement: the noaa.gov/disclaimer page read is only a links disclaimer; no public-domain statement was located. SciJinks is a joint NOAA/NASA site historically produced at JPL [BK], which would raise the Caltech-contractor issue; unverified.
- Not checked this session (no statement read): NIST, CBO, Library of Congress "Everyday Mysteries" (page returned no parsable text), National Park Service, FDA consumer updates, NSF research news, Census "America Counts", plainlanguage.gov examples. Their status follows the general section 105 rule only in principle.
- Whether NASA article text written by contractor science writers is a section 105 work: not answered by NASA's page.
- The Feynman Lectures site's own terms page could not be read (403); ownership and terms rest on secondary reports plus the robots.txt signal.
- Smithsonian's status under section 105 (trust instrumentality) was not confirmed by any source found.
- US court opinions and patents: not researched. [BK]: federal judicial opinions are government works (edicts of government); patents' text is generally unrestricted; neither register fits the goal well.

## 2. Register: how plain, concrete, modern and lively is the prose? House style?

### Takeaway
Agency explainers are plain, short-sentenced, second-person and concrete, with a legally mandated plain-language house style, but they are flat and impersonal; they would teach clarity and the absence of AI tics rather than a voice. CRS is denser, neutral and analytic (closest to "news-style summary"); USGS Water Science School and EIA Energy Explained are the closest to science and technical walkthroughs.

### Cited Findings
- Plain Writing Act of 2010 (signed 13 October 2010) requires agencies to use clear communication the public can understand and use; agencies were directed to follow the Federal Plain Language Guidelines. [S] — [digital.gov: requirements for plain writing](https://digital.gov/resources/plain-writing-act); [plainlanguage.gov law page](https://plainlanguage.gov/law)
- In September 2025 GSA redirected plainlanguage.gov to digital.gov guides; the Center for Plain Language says those guides are not the Federal Plain Language Guidelines and has reposted the last full version. [S] — [Center for Plain Language](https://centerforplainlanguage.org/the-federal-plain-language-guidelines-are-missing/)
- **USGS Water Science School** sample (dated 8 June 2019, byline "Water Science School", roughly 900 to 1,000 words): "Evaporation occurs when energy (heat) forces the bonds that hold water molecules together to break. When you're boiling water on the stove, you're adding heat to liquid water. This added heat breaks the bonds, causing the water to shift from its liquid state to its gaseous state (water vapor), which we know as steam." Traits: second person, kitchen-scale example, one idea per sentence, parenthetical glosses. [P] — [USGS: Evaporation and the Water Cycle](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/evaporation-and-water-cycle)
- **EIA Energy Explained** sample (last updated 31 October 2023, about 1,200 words): "An electric generator is a device that converts a form of energy into electricity. ... He found that moving a magnet inside a coil of wire makes (induces) an electric current flow through the wire." Traits: definitional openers, mechanism described step by step, technical terms introduced with an em-dash gloss ("an electromagnet—a magnet produced by electricity—"), no figurative language, somewhat textbook-flat. [P] — [EIA: How electricity is generated](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php)
- **MedlinePlus health topic** sample: "Asthma is a chronic (long-term) lung disease. It affects your airways, the tubes that carry air in and out of your lungs. When you have asthma, your airways can become inflamed and narrowed." Traits: very short sentences, "you/your", jargon immediately glossed, heavy use of bulleted lists after a lead-in sentence ending in a colon. [P] — [MedlinePlus: Asthma](https://medlineplus.gov/asthma.html)
- **CRS In Focus** sample (IF10244, "Wildfire Statistics", versions 23 June 2015 to 1 June 2023, about 500 to 600 words of body text): opens with a definition of wildfire and a statement of which level of government responds on which lands; neutral, statistic-led, institutional. [P] — [EveryCRSReport IF10244](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF10244.html)
- **USA.gov** sample: "Not everything that appears on a federal government website is a government work." Short declaratives, imperative guidance. [P] — [usa.gov/government-copyright](https://www.usa.gov/government-copyright)

### Inferences
- Shared traits across the agency samples: active voice, present tense, "you", glossed terms, no hedging stacks, no rhetorical flourishes, no first-person voice, little humour or narrative. This is the opposite of generic LLM prose in diction (no "delve", no triads, no summarising closers) but it is institutionally bland, so "readers prefer it" is not guaranteed.
- Structural risk: MedlinePlus, CDC and similar consumer-health pages are list-heavy and fragmentary; a model trained on them will learn bullets and "lead-in sentence + colon" patterns. Prefer sources with continuous paragraphs (CRS, Water Science School, Energy Explained, Earth Observatory, BLS Beyond the Numbers, VOA Learning English).
- EIA's own prose uses em-dash glosses, so a corpus built from it will not suppress em-dashes if that is a style goal.
- Liveliness ranking, from samples read and [BK] for those not sampled: NASA Earth Observatory features and VOA Learning English science stories (narrative, quoted scientists) > USGS Water Science School > NIH News in Health > EIA / MedlinePlus / BLS OOH > CRS > GAO highlights.
- VOA Learning English is written to a controlled vocabulary and short sentences for learners [BK]; it may over-simplify relative to the target register and should be a minority share.

### Gaps
- No sample text could be read from NASA science pages (navigation chrome only was extracted), NIH News in Health (blocked), Library of Congress Everyday Mysteries, BLS OOH (bot-policy interstitial), NOAA, NIST, Census, NPS, FDA or NSF. Register judgments for those are [BK] only.
- No readability scores (Flesch-Kincaid etc.) were computed for any source.
- Did not confirm the current legal force of the Federal Plain Language Guidelines after the 2025 site change.

## 3. Size and official bulk access

### Takeaway
CRS is the only candidate with a verified, purpose-built bulk download (23,620 reports with per-version dates); it alone clears 300,000 words many times over. Most other single agency explainer sets are small (hundreds of pages of about 1,000 words) and need to be pooled; their sizes were not measured.

### Cited Findings
- "EveryCRSReport.com includes 23,620 CRS reports", including all reports on CRS.gov plus about 5,100 archived reports from University of North Texas Libraries; analyst phone numbers and emails are redacted. [P] — [EveryCRSReport about](https://www.everycrsreport.com/about.html)
- Bulk access: "All of the CRS reports (and metadata) on this site is available for download." A single `reports.csv` lists `number, url, sha1, latestPubDate, title, latestPDF, latestHTML`; per-report JSON has a `versions` array with `date`, `title`, `summary`, `formats` (HTML/PDF), `topics`; a reference `bulk-download.py` script is provided. No rate-limit guidance is stated. [P] — [EveryCRSReport download](https://www.everycrsreport.com/download.html)
- MedlinePlus offers downloadable XML files and a web service for developers. [P] — [MedlinePlus: using content](https://medlineplus.gov/about/using/usingcontent/)
- Sitemaps advertised in robots.txt: science.nasa.gov (`wp-sitemap.xml`), medlineplus.gov, learningenglish.voanews.com, eia.gov, bls.gov, congress.gov. [P] — [science.nasa.gov robots](https://science.nasa.gov/robots.txt); [VOA LE robots](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/robots.txt); [EIA robots](https://www.eia.gov/robots.txt)
- NCI: "Some of our content is also available for syndication". [P] — [cancer.gov copyright and reuse](https://www.cancer.gov/policies/copyright-reuse)
- Earth Observatory now lives under science.nasa.gov (earthobservatory.nasa.gov resolves to `science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/`), with page metadata showing first publication 29 April 1999. [P] — [Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/)
- Typical item lengths seen: Water Science School page about 900 to 1,000 words; Energy Explained page about 1,200 words; CRS In Focus about 500 to 600 words (all tool estimates). [P] — sample pages cited in section 2

### Inferences
- CRS: even if the average report were only 3,000 words, 23,620 reports would be on the order of 70 million words; the 300,000-word floor is met by a few hundred In Focus pieces plus short reports. Selection, not volume, is the problem.
- Earth Observatory has published daily image stories since 1999, so its total is very likely in the millions of words [BK for daily cadence]; VOA Learning English has published several items daily for many years, likewise millions of words [BK].
- Water Science School, Energy Explained, NIH News in Health and Everyday Mysteries are each probably in the 100,000 to 500,000 word range [BK estimate], so each is marginal alone but useful pooled.

### Gaps
- No word totals or item counts were measured for any source other than the CRS report count. All other size figures are estimates.
- Whether EveryCRSReport metadata distinguishes "In Focus" from full reports: the documented example shows `"type": "CRS Report"` only; In Focus items are identifiable by the `IF` number prefix (seen in IF10244) but a type field was not confirmed.
- Did not check data.gov, GitHub mirrors, or agency APIs (NASA, USGS, Census, BLS) for text endpoints.
- congress.gov's own CRS bulk access terms were not read (403).

## 4. Dates: filtering out post-November-2022 text; signs of LLM drafting

### Takeaway
Date filtering is easy for dated serials (CRS versions, Earth Observatory, VOA, NIH News in Health, BLS Beyond the Numbers) and unreliable for evergreen explainer pages, which carry only a "last updated" date and are revised in place. No evidence was found that any specific agency page is LLM-drafted, but federal generative-AI use has grown fast since 2023, so a hard pre-November-2022 cut is the prudent default.

### Cited Findings
- CRS: each report's JSON lists every version with its own date, so a version dated before November 2022 can be selected even for reports updated later. [P] — [EveryCRSReport download](https://www.everycrsreport.com/download.html)
- EIA Energy Explained page shows "Last updated: October 31, 2023", i.e. an evergreen page revised after the cutoff with no original-publication date exposed. [P] — [EIA sample](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php)
- USGS Water Science School page carries a 8 June 2019 date. [P] — [USGS sample](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/evaporation-and-water-cycle)
- science.nasa.gov pages expose `article:published_time` and `article:modified_time` metadata. [P] — [Earth Observatory landing page](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/)
- GAO reported that agencies' generative-AI use cases increased about ninefold from 2023 to 2024. [S] — [GAO-25-107653](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107653/index.html)
- HHS's civil-rights office disclosed using ChatGPT to break down legal concepts in plain language (in investigations work, not stated to be public web pages). [S] — [FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/hhs-reported-ai-uses-soar-including-pilots-to-address-staff-shortage/)
- Source continuity problems after 2025: climate.gov stopped publishing 1 July 2025 [S] — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/06/12/nx-s1-5431660/climate-us-government-website-changes); the World Factbook ended 4 February 2026 [S] — [WTOP/AP](https://wtop.com/news/2026/02/cia-ends-publication-of-its-popular-world-factbook-reference-tool); VOA operations were largely halted from March 2025, a district judge ordered staff reinstated in March 2026 and an appeals panel stayed that order on 31 March 2026 [S] — [Washington Times, 31 March 2026](https://m.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/mar/31/appeals-court-blocks-lower-judges-return-work-order-voice-america/); [JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/03/us-federal-judge-orders-voice-of-america-broadcasting-restored/)

### Inferences
- For evergreen pages (Energy Explained, MedlinePlus topics, NASA "What is" pages, USGS FAQs) the clean way to get pre-November-2022 text is a dated snapshot (for example an Internet Archive capture from before that date) rather than the live page; the live "last updated" date cannot prove the text predates the cutoff.
- Because the sources are archival and several have stopped publishing, nothing is lost by cutting at November 2022.

### Gaps
- No agency AI-use-case inventory entry for "drafting public web content" was found; absence of evidence only. The OMB consolidated inventory was not opened.
- Current (November 2026) publishing status of VOA Learning English is unconfirmed; search results showed podcast listings dated to March 2026 and one report that the VOA website had not been updated since mid-March 2026.
- Whether the learningenglish.voanews.com archive will stay online is unknown.

## 5. Pre-1930 science and mathematics writers

### Takeaway
The US public-domain cutoff is confirmed: everything published in 1930 or earlier is public domain as of 1 January 2026. The candidate authors are all eligible, but their prose is a century old and heavily memorized by base models; they are best used as a small supplementary slice (Thompson, Russell's ABC books, Eddington, Haldane), not the core corpus. Per-work word counts and Gutenberg availability were mostly not verified this session.

### Cited Findings
- "January 1, 2026 is Public Domain Day: Works from 1930 are open to all, as are sound recordings from 1925!" The Duke list names Bertrand Russell's *The Conquest of Happiness* (1930) and Freud's *Civilization and Its Discontents* among the entrants. [P] — [Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain, 2026](https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2026/)
- *Calculus Made Easy* by Silvanus P. Thompson is on Project Gutenberg (ebook 33283, released 28 July 2010), marked "Public domain in the USA", with 25,575 downloads in the prior 30 days; the page lists no word count. [P] — [Project Gutenberg 33283](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33283)

### Inferences
- With a 1930 cutoff, every work named in the brief (Faraday, Darwin, Huxley, Tyndall, Clifford, Poincare translations of 1905 to 1914, Thompson 1910, Russell 1912/1919/1923/1925, William James, Wells's pre-1931 nonfiction, Eddington and Haldane titles through 1930) is public domain by publication date, provided the specific edition and, for Poincare, the specific English translation was itself published by 1930. Publication years are as given in the brief or [BK].
- Additional eligible candidates [BK, unverified]: Eddington, *Stars and Atoms* (1927) and *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928); James Jeans, *The Universe Around Us* (1929) and *The Mysterious Universe* (1930); Haldane, *Daedalus* (1924) and *Possible Worlds* (1927); Russell, *The Conquest of Happiness* (1930, confirmed by Duke).
- Register [BK judgment]: closest to modern plain explanation are Thompson's *Calculus Made Easy* (direct address, jokes, worked steps), Russell's *ABC of Atoms* and *ABC of Relativity*, Haldane's essays, and Eddington's and Jeans's late-1920s popular books. Faraday (lecture transcript, 1860s), Darwin, Huxley, Tyndall, Clifford and William James have long periodic sentences, Victorian diction and British spelling, and would pull an 8B model toward an archaic register.
- Pre-1930 science content is also factually obsolete (pre-quantum-mechanics consolidation, pre-DNA), which matters if the model is expected to explain modern topics; style transfer may carry outdated framing.
- The high Gutenberg download count for *Calculus Made Easy* signals the popularity that correlates with memorization (see section 6).

### Gaps
- Word counts per work: none verified (Gutenberg catalogue pages do not list them, and texts were deliberately not downloaded). Rough [BK] orders of magnitude only: *Chemical History of a Candle* about 40k to 50k; *Calculus Made Easy* about 50k to 60k; *Problems of Philosophy* about 45k; *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* about 70k; *ABC of Relativity* and *ABC of Atoms* about 40k to 50k each; *Origin of Species* about 150k to 200k. Treat as unverified.
- Presence of clean Gutenberg texts was verified only for *Calculus Made Easy*. [BK]: Faraday's *Candle*, Darwin, Huxley, Tyndall, Russell's *Problems of Philosophy* and *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*, Poincare's *Science and Hypothesis*, Clifford and James are on Gutenberg; whether *ABC of Atoms*, *ABC of Relativity*, Eddington, Jeans and Haldane titles have Gutenberg (as opposed to Internet Archive scan) editions was not checked.
- Gutenberg math texts such as *Calculus Made Easy* are distributed with LaTeX/PDF sources [BK]; the cleanliness of a plain-text version for training was not checked.
- 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica: not researched this session. [BK]: public domain, very large, dense and dated encyclopedic register, OCR quality varies.

## 6. Memorization by base LLMs

### Takeaway
Published work shows verbatim extraction of Project Gutenberg books from Llama-family models rises with a book's popularity, so the famous pre-1930 titles are the most likely to be memorized; no study was found on memorization of government web text.

### Cited Findings
- "Memorization: A Close Look at Books" (2025) tested Llama 3 and 3.1 on Project Gutenberg books and found extraction rates correlate with book popularity (number of ratings), read as a sign of duplication in training data; instruction tuning reduced extraction, and fine-tuning could partly undo that. [S] — [arXiv 2504.12549](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12549)
- A related study found verbatim recall is most strongly triggered by prefixes from the start of a document and falls sharply at offsets. [S] — [arXiv 2505.13171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13171)
- A study of open-weight models on Books3 found most models do not memorize most books, but Llama 3.1 70B memorized some very popular ones nearly entirely. [S] — [arXiv 2505.12546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12546)

### Inferences
- For a style fine-tune, memorized training text is a nuisance rather than a legal problem here (it is public domain), but it distorts held-out evaluation: loss on *Origin of Species* or *Problems of Philosophy* will look better than the style learning warrants. Dev/test splits should come from low-popularity material.
- Government pages are almost certainly in base-model pretraining data too (they are open, heavily linked, and in Common Crawl) [BK], but individual CRS In Focus pieces or Water Science School pages are far less duplicated than Gutenberg classics, so verbatim memorization should be lower. This is an inference; no measurement was found.

### Gaps
- No source measured memorization of CRS, NASA, NIH, USGS or VOA text.
- The cited papers were seen as search summaries, not read in full; details (models, thresholds) should be checked before quoting numbers.

## 7. robots.txt and bulk-access etiquette

### Takeaway
Most agency sites allow general crawling with sitemaps, but congress.gov blocks a long list of AI crawlers, BLS prohibits non-conforming bots, FRED and the Feynman site forbid scraping or AI training outright, and nih.gov sites sit behind bot protection. Use official bulk channels (EveryCRSReport download, MedlinePlus XML) rather than scraping.

### Cited Findings

## 8. One-line verdict per source, and the strongest 3 to 5

### Takeaway
Strongest public-domain options, in order: (1) CRS reports via EveryCRSReport, pre-November-2022 versions, weighted toward In Focus and short reports; (2) a pooled "agency explainer" set of USGS Water Science School and FAQs, EIA Energy Explained and MedlinePlus health-topic summaries; (3) NASA Earth Observatory and NASA Science articles, subject to the contractor caveat; (4) VOA Learning English science and technology stories with wire-derived items removed; (5) a small pre-1930 supplement (Thompson, Russell's ABC books, Eddington, Haldane). All verdicts below are the researcher's judgments resting on the findings in sections 1 to 7.

### Cited Findings
- Verdict inputs are the cited findings above; the key discriminating facts are: CRS bulk download with 23,620 reports and per-version dates — [EveryCRSReport](https://www.everycrsreport.com/about.html); FRED's ban on ML training — [FRED legal](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/legal/); Feynman site's `ai-train=no` — [robots.txt](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/robots.txt); MedlinePlus A.D.A.M./ASHP exclusions — [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/about/using/usingcontent/); VOA "produced exclusively" limit and AP exclusion — [VOA terms](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/p/6021.html); CDC contractor/grantee restriction — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/other/agencymaterials.html); climate.gov contractor team — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/06/12/nx-s1-5431660/climate-us-government-website-changes); 1930 cutoff — [Duke](https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2026/).

### Inferences

**A. US government**
- CRS reports and In Focus: best overall; clear status, huge, dated, bulk download; neutral analytic register, dry, policy-heavy topic mix.
- GAO highlights: clear status; formulaic "What GAO Found" bureaucratic register; low value.
- CBO explainers: status and register unverified; likely dry budget prose; skip unless needed.
- USGS Water Science School and FAQs: clear status, plain concrete science walkthroughs; small; good pooled component.
- EIA Energy Explained: clear status, solid technical explainer register, flat; evergreen pages need archived pre-2022 snapshots.
- MedlinePlus health-topic summaries: clear status for NLM-written summaries only; very plain but list-heavy; exclude A.D.A.M. encyclopedia and drug monographs.
- NCI About Cancer: text stated free of copyright; plain patient register; narrow domain.
- NIH News in Health, Research Matters, NIGMS Biomedical Beat: probably good, lively consumer science; status and access unverified (site blocks scripts).
- CDC plain-language pages: mostly public domain but contractor/grantee exceptions and fragmentary list style; low priority.
- NASA Earth Observatory and NASA Science: best narrative science prose among agencies and large; text status very probable but not explicitly stated for contractor-written text; exclude JPL/Caltech-authored material.
- NOAA SciJinks, Ocean Service: status not verified; SciJinks is for children; low priority.
- climate.gov: contractor-written and discontinued; avoid unless status is cleared.
- NIST, NSF news, FDA consumer updates, Census America Counts, NPS articles, LoC Everyday Mysteries: not verified; plausible small additions under the general rule.
- BLS Beyond the Numbers and OOH: clear status; OOH is templated and repetitive, Beyond the Numbers is decent statistical explanation; bot policy applies.
- Federal Reserve Board education pages: public domain per the Board; small.
- FRED blog and all Reserve Bank content: exclude; copyrighted and ML training prohibited.
- Smithsonian: exclude except CC0 items; text is not generally public domain.
- VOA Learning English: public domain when VOA-exclusive; very plain, modern, news-style; over-simplified vocabulary; filter wire-derived stories; archive availability uncertain.
- plainlanguage.gov examples: site redirected in 2025; tiny; useful as a style reference, not a corpus.

**B. Pre-1930**
- Thompson, *Calculus Made Easy*: best single pre-1930 fit for mathematical walkthroughs; small; heavily downloaded and likely memorized.
- Russell (*ABC of Atoms*, *ABC of Relativity*, *Problems of Philosophy*, *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*): clearest prose of the group; British, somewhat formal; likely memorized.
- Eddington, Jeans, Haldane (1920s): nearest to modern popular science; Gutenberg availability unverified.
- Faraday, Darwin, Huxley, Tyndall, Clifford, Poincare (translations), William James, Wells: public domain but too dated in syntax and diction to anchor a modern register; use sparingly or not at all.

**C. Other**
- Feynman Lectures: not public domain; free to read only; AI training expressly refused; exclude.
- 1911 Britannica: public domain but archaic and encyclopedic; exclude.
- CIA World Factbook: discontinued February 2026; telegraphic fact-list register; exclude.
- Court opinions and patents: wrong register; exclude.

**Cross-cutting caveats for the report writer**
- None of these sources has a single author's voice; a government corpus teaches a house style (clear, impersonal). If the project needs a distinctive voice that readers prefer, public-domain material mainly offers a clean "plain baseline", not personality.
- A mixed corpus will mix registers (policy analysis, consumer health, science features, learner English); consistency needs a deliberate weighting, for example science and technical explainers as the majority and CRS In Focus for the news-summary form.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head reader-preference evidence exists for any of these registers against generic AI writing; the "readers prefer" criterion is untested.
- Verdicts for sources marked unverified above (NIH serials, NOAA, NIST, NSF, FDA, Census, NPS, LoC, CBO) rest on the general section 105 rule and background knowledge, not on pages read this session.
