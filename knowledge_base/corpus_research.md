# eLife digests should anchor quill's corpus

The best primary corpus is the set of eLife digests: about 1.68 million words of professionally edited plain-language science explanation under CC BY 4.0, written before March 2022, in complete 350-word units that need no chunking. It is the only candidate that scores well on all seven criteria, and its one real weakness is topic: it is biomedicine only, so it cannot teach news summaries or mathematics walkthroughs by itself. The recommended blend therefore keeps eLife at roughly 35% and adds Our World in Data articles (20%), filtered Stack Exchange answers for mathematics and technical walkthroughs (20%), US government explainers (15%) and open-licence news (10%). The Stack Exchange slice is CC BY-SA, which on Creative Commons' own reading means a published adapter should carry CC BY-SA; every other slice needs attribution only or nothing. The research also shows that ordinary readers do not prefer human informational writing to LLM writing in blind tests, while expert readers and frequent LLM users do, so success has to be defined as a blind pairwise win against vanilla Claude with a named reader pool. No researcher downloaded or read any corpus at length: every register judgement rests on descriptions and one or two sample pages, most word counts are computed or estimated, and several licence pages were seen only through a summarising fetch tool or a search snippet. The hand checks in the last section come before any crawling or downloading.

## Five corpora survive the licence filter, and eLife ranks first

### How the licence terms map to storage, training and publishing

Creative Commons' guidance separates three acts. A private local fine-tune shares nothing, so attribution, ShareAlike and NoDerivatives conditions are not triggered and only NonCommercial applies; publishing sample outputs matters only if they reproduce source text; publishing adapter weights is the act that brings ShareAlike and NonCommercial into play. CC states that NonCommercial covers "all stages, from copying the data during training to sharing the trained model", that following NoDerivatives "would require that ND-licensed content not be used as training data", and that ShareAlike would require the same licence on a publicly shared model ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/)). CC also says its licences bind only where copyright permission is needed and that its guidance "is likely to lead to overcompliance" ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/)). Whether weights are an adaptation of training text is unsettled, and no source the researchers retrieved resolves it; MIT OpenCourseWare's terms show at least one licensor treats trained models as derivative works ([MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/pages/privacy-and-terms-of-use/)). US case law does not settle it either: two 2025 district rulings found generative training fair use, and the first appellate ruling, in September 2026, rejected fair use for a non-generative tool while leaving the generative question open ([Ballard Spahr](https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2026/10/third-circuit-addresses-fair-use-in-ai-training-but-leaves-generative-ai-questions-unresolved)). None of this is legal advice. The working rule for this report is the conservative one: **public domain, CC0 and CC BY leave adapter release unencumbered; CC BY-SA is workable if the adapter is released under CC BY-SA with attribution; NC and ND sources stay out of anything that might be published.**

### The ranked shortlist

**1. eLife digests.** eLife's terms state that journal content is under a Creative Commons Attribution licence, linked to CC BY 4.0, "except where otherwise noted" ([eLife terms](https://elifesciences.org/terms)); the terms page does not name digests separately, so their coverage is an inference from their being part of each article. The published lay-summary dataset holds **4,828 digests averaging 347.6 words**, written by expert editors from the article and the author's answers, retrieved in March 2022, with a Flesch-Kincaid grade of 10.92 against 14.76 for PLOS author summaries ([Goldsack et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.09932)). That is about 1.68 million words (the researchers' multiplication, not a published figure), five times the project's floor. The voice is a house style from a small editorial team working to a published guide ([eLife](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/85518309/plain-language-summaries-how-to-write-an-elife-digest)), and the one sample page read showed active, jargon-cutting prose with playful headlines ([eLife digests](https://elifesciences.org/digests)). Each digest appears once on the web, so verbatim memorization should be lower than for famous texts; that is an inference, since no study measures it. Extraction is the easiest of any candidate: a Hugging Face dataset whose card says CC BY 4.0 ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tomasg25/scientific_lay_summarisation)) and an official XML repository ([elife-article-xml](https://github.com/elifesciences/elife-article-xml)). The risks are the narrow domain and a recognisable digest formula.

**2. Our World in Data.** The article footer and FAQ both state CC BY for all articles, and the FAQ adds "You can republish or reproduce our writing, online or in print, for free and without permission" ([OWID FAQ](https://ourworldindata.org/faqs)). It is the best register match found: the one article sampled has short declarative sentences, first-person voice and precise numbers restated in everyday units ([OWID sample](https://ourworldindata.org/much-better-awful-can-be-better)), and named bylines allow filtering to two or three authors for a near single voice. Its problems are practical. No text dump exists, since the content repository holds only explorer configurations and was archived in April 2025 ([owid-content](https://github.com/owid/owid-content)), so it would need a page-by-page fetch. Its size was not measured. Articles are revised in place (the sample was first written in 2018, published in 2022 and revised in 2024), so a publication date does not prove pre-December-2022 text. The FAQ also carries the line "You must not edit the material", which sits oddly beside a CC BY grant, third-party data is excluded from the licence, and the robots.txt fetch returned only a sitemap line and was probably truncated.

**3. US government explainers.** Text by federal employees has no US copyright under 17 U.S.C. 105 ([copyright.gov](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html)), which removes every licence obligation. The usable set is CRS reports, especially the 500-to-600-word In Focus pieces, plus USGS Water Science School and EIA Energy Explained, each with a quoted public-domain statement on its agency page ([EveryCRSReport](https://www.everycrsreport.com/download.html); [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/information-policies-and-instructions/copyrights-and-credits); [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/about/copyrights_reuse.php)). CRS has a purpose-built bulk download of **23,620 reports with a date on every version**, so a pre-December-2022 version can be chosen exactly ([EveryCRSReport](https://www.everycrsreport.com/about.html)). The samples read are plain, concrete and second-person, and also flat and impersonal; CRS is dry and policy-heavy. This slice teaches clarity, not a voice. Evergreen pages such as EIA's show only a "last updated" date, so pre-2022 text needs a dated snapshot. The CRS public-domain statement was read on EveryCRSReport, a third-party host; congress.gov's own page refused the fetch.

**4. Stack Exchange answers, filtered, from the Common Pile copy.** This is the only open source found for mathematics and technical walkthroughs. Common Pile carries per-document licence metadata (CC BY-SA 3.0 or 4.0), a `created` field for date filtering, and answers ordered by votes ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/common-pile/stackexchange)). The filter the researchers propose is accepted or top-voted answers of 150 to 800 words, created before December 2022, from prose-heavy sites such as Mathematics, Physics and Cross Validated, with questions and comments dropped. It ranks fourth because ShareAlike constrains adapter release, authorship is thousands of people, quality varies, famous answers are likely memorized, and the filtering is real work. Two things were not verified: the Stack Exchange licensing page could not be opened, and since July 2024 the official dump terms say a recipient who trains an LLM can be denied future access, which critics say conflicts with CC BY-SA ([DevClass](https://www.devclass.com/development/2024/07/30/stack-exchange-restricts-access-to-dump-of-user-contributed-data-critics-complain-this-contradicts-license/1625192)). The share of prose against formula and code per site was not measured.

**5. Open-licence news: Wikinews plus an outlet allowlist.** English Wikinews has **9,985,755 words** ([Wikinews statistics](https://en.wikinews.org/wiki/Special:Statistics)) under CC BY 2.5 for 2005 to December 2024 and CC BY 4.0 after ([Wikinews:Copyright](https://en.wikinews.org/wiki/Wikinews:Copyright)), with official dumps. It supplies the news-summary shape, but the researchers judge its prose flat and attribution-heavy from prior familiarity, without sampling. The livelier candidates are 360info (CC BY 4.0, academics edited into plain language by journalists) and Global Voices (CC BY 3.0), both verified on the publishers' own pages ([360info](https://360info.org/about-us/); [Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/about/global-voices-attribution-policy/)), and available with per-document licence fields in `common-pile/news` ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/common-pile/news)). Per-outlet word counts are unpublished, preview articles date from 2021 to 2024, the publication date is in the text and not a clean field, and the set includes state media and fact-check boilerplate that an allowlist must remove.

Two single-author corpora are usable today and sit outside the top five on purpose. Slate Star Codex (2013 to January 2021) is CC BY 4.0 per its About page ([SSC](https://slatestarcodex.com/about/)), and Gwern.net is CC0 with an `ai-train=yes` signal in robots.txt and more than 4.38 million words as of November 2022 ([gwern.net/about](https://gwern.net/about); [robots.txt](https://gwern.net/robots.txt)). Both are opinionated or dense essay registers, which repeats the problem the previous corpus had, and both are among the most-read blogs on the web, so base models have very likely memorized them. They are fallbacks if a single voice turns out to matter more than register.

## Start with eLife alone, then blend at roughly 35/20/20/15/10

The primary recommendation is eLife digests alone as the first experiment. It isolates the question the project most needs answered, which is whether an edited explanatory house style gives readers something to prefer, and it does so with the cleanest licence, the lowest contamination and the least engineering. Digest length matters here: the fine-tuning study with the strongest reader-preference result used excerpts of 250 to 650 words, each paired with a content description as input ([Chakrabarty et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v3)), and the one direct demonstration on Qwen3 used about 12,000 pairs mapping an LLM paraphrase back to the human original with one LoRA epoch ([arXiv 2605.19516](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19516v1)). A digest is a complete explainer with its own opening and close, so it fits either format without cutting passages out of the middle of long documents.

The recommended blend, for a corpus of 300,000 to 400,000 words, is **eLife digests 35%, Our World in Data 20%, Stack Exchange mathematics and technical answers 20%, US government explainers 15%, open-licence news 10%**. eLife stays the largest share because it is the most coherent voice and the best-verified source. OWID gets the second share because it is the one candidate with visible life in the prose and a handful of named authors; its share is a ceiling, and it falls to zero if the hand checks or the measured size disappoint. Stack Exchange is there for the mathematics walkthroughs no CC BY source covers, capped at a fifth because it is the most heterogeneous slice. Government explainers supply technical and policy explanation with no licence strings, capped so that institutional phrasing ("GAO was asked to review") does not become the model's default. News is the smallest slice because its best-licensed source is its flattest. Each training example should carry a source or genre tag, since exemplar or tag conditioning is the published way to keep a many-author model steerable ([arXiv 2505.14608](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14608v2)). These proportions are the report's judgement; no study tests blend ratios.

The Stack Exchange slice changes the licence posture of the whole adapter. With it, the conservative course is to release the adapter under CC BY-SA 4.0 with an attribution list. Without it, every source is CC BY or public domain and the adapter needs attribution only. If CC BY-SA on the weights is unwelcome, replace that 20% with more USGS and EIA text and the small CC BY technical sources (The Carpentries lessons, Explained from First Principles, Distill), accept weaker mathematics coverage, and ask Kalid Azad for permission to use Better Explained, which is the best mathematics register found and already invites contact for uses beyond its non-commercial licence ([Better Explained](https://betterexplained.com/about/)). The notes flag this gap plainly: **no mathematics-walkthrough source with a clean CC BY licence and good prose was confirmed.**

## Lay readers do not prefer human explainers, so name the readers

### One author gives a voice; a broad mix gives neutrality; house style is untested

Direct evidence on author count is thin. Per-author fine-tuning produced text that readers preferred regardless of corpus size, from two books to twenty-two, with a correlation between size and preference below 0.1 ([Chakrabarty et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v3)). At the other end, fine-tuning an instruct model on FineWeb-Edu, a broad set of unauthored educational web text, recovered much of the human-likeness that detectors measure ([arXiv 2605.19516](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19516v1)); that result says nothing about reader preference. A 2026 preprint reports that naively pooling authors blends opposing style directions into "author-style homogenization", though the researchers saw that claim only in a search snippet ([arXiv 2609.30968](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.30968)). **No study compares one author, a house style and a broad mix on reader preference, and none tests whether a voice learned on narrative essays carries into technical explanation.** The project's own experience fits a finding from the Biber-feature literature, that a style suited to one register is conspicuous in another ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). The reasonable reading is that register match matters more than author count, that a house-style corpus is the middle case where editing keeps style directions aligned, and that this is a hypothesis the eLife-only run tests directly. Data volume is not the constraint: the working data sizes in the literature bracket the project's 300,000 words.

### Blind tests favour LLM text with lay readers and human text with experts

On informational text, ordinary readers mostly do not prefer human writing. GPT-4 lay summaries of PNAS papers were rated clearer than the researchers' own with authorship hidden (snippet-level) ([Markowitz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.00706)); 150 crowd workers rated LLM plain-language summaries indistinguishable from human ones, though the human ones produced significantly better comprehension ([Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10409v1)); and a preregistered study of 599 people found no quality difference between journalist-written and AI-generated news ([Gilardi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.03500v2)). Preference for human text appears with trained readers: against prompted frontier models, 28 MFA readers strongly preferred human text while 131 lay readers leaned toward the AI on quality, and after per-author fine-tuning both groups preferred the fine-tuned output, with Pangram's flag rate falling from 97% to 3% ([Chakrabarty et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v3)). That study is literary fiction on GPT-4o, not exposition on an 8B model. Frequent LLM users are the sensitive population: a majority vote of five misclassified 1 of 300 non-fiction articles ([Russell et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267)). The researchers found no blind pairwise study of human against LLM technical explainers or mathematics walkthroughs.

### What this implies for the evaluation against vanilla Claude

"Readers clearly prefer it" is a hard bar with naive readers and a reachable one with heavy LLM users, so the claim must name its pool and report the two groups separately. The primary metric should be blind, randomized pairwise judgements on the same content: quill's version against vanilla Claude's version of the same material, length-matched, no labels, left and right shuffled. Labels alone move preference, with raters favouring whichever text is tagged human (snippet-level) ([Zhu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03723v2.pdf)). By the researchers' own power calculation (two-sided binomial test against 50%, 5% significance, 80% power, which this report re-derived), detecting a true 60% preference needs about 195 independent judgements, 65% needs about 85, and 55% needs about 780; correlated judgements from the same rater or item make these lower bounds, so the test should spread over at least 30 to 40 source items and be analysed with a mixed model or a bootstrap over items. A sensible pre-registered threshold is a lower confidence bound above 50% with a point estimate of at least 60%.

Three guardrails follow from the evidence. Track comprehension and factual fidelity alongside preference, because ratings and comprehension diverged in the plain-language study and a restyler can lose by removing clarity. Do not use an LLM judge as the headline: judges rate lower-perplexity, familiar text higher even without knowing whose it is ([Wataoka et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21819v1)), which biases them toward the generic arm, and a Claude judge is ruled out by being one of the arms. Treat detector and slop scores as development diagnostics only, since a model tuned to beat a token-statistics detector pushed it below chance while a style detector still scored about 0.95 ([arXiv 2505.14608](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14608v2)). The most durable diagnostics are grammatical rates against a human reference: GPT-4o uses present-participial clauses at 5.3 times and nominalizations at 2.1 times the human rate ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)), whereas marker words such as "delve" fade within a year or two of becoming famous ([Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

### Memorization argues against famous text and for a recall probe

Fine-tuning can reactivate text the base model memorized in pretraining. GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek V3.1, fine-tuned to expand plot summaries, reproduced up to 85 to 90% of held-out books, and fine-tuning on one author unlocked recall of more than thirty unrelated ones ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20957)). That work covers large hosted models; the effect at 8B is unknown, and one unreviewed preprint found near-zero memorization of fine-tuning data under LoRA ([Wang & Li](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20856v1)). Extraction of Gutenberg books from Llama models rises with a book's popularity (snippet-level) ([arXiv 2504.12549](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12549)). Qwen3 names no specific pretraining dataset ([Qwen3 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09388)), so every candidate here should be assumed seen. The practical consequences are to avoid canonical classics and famous essays, hold out evaluation passages, keep epochs low, and repeat the project's recall probe with an n-gram overlap check of outputs against the corpus. A restyler has a built-in safeguard in that content comes from the user's draft.

## The best-written candidates are closed, non-commercial or no-derivatives

The sources a writer would pick first mostly fail the first criterion. Quanta's terms forbid storing, scraping and data mining ([Quanta terms](https://www.quantamagazine.org/terms-conditions/)); Stratechery's terms ban data mining and republication ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/terms-of-service/)); 3Blue1Brown reserves all rights over its written lessons ([License.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/3b1b/3Blue1Brown.com/main/License.md)); Aeon sells republication and Undark uses its own revocable terms ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/republish); [Undark](https://undark.org/republishing-guidelines/)). The Feynman Lectures site signals `ai-train=no` in robots.txt ([robots.txt](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/robots.txt)), and FRED's terms prohibit use in training any machine-learning system ([FRED legal](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/legal/)). Khan Academy is out on a help-centre guideline against AI training that the researchers saw only as a search snippet ([Khan Academy](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/de/articles/42929097425037)). Two authors have objected in their own words and should be respected whatever the law allows: Julia Evans' robots.txt reads "NO LLM PLZ" ([jvns.ca](https://jvns.ca/robots.txt)), and Jeremy Kun's About page forbids AI use of his content ([jeremykun.com](https://www.jeremykun.com/about/)). Paul Graham, Dan Luu, Simon Willison, Ciechanowski, Red Blob Games, Astral Codex Ten and a dozen similar sites show no licence on the pages checked and are all rights reserved by default; the "MIT-licensed" Paul Graham dataset on Hugging Face states no permission from the author ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/sgoel9/paul_graham_essays)). Terence Tao asks to be contacted before large copying ([Tao](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/about/)).

No-derivatives and non-commercial licences rule out a second group for any published adapter. The Conversation is CC BY-ND, bars systematic republication and blocks AI crawlers in robots.txt ([guidelines](https://theconversation.com/us/republishing-guidelines); [robots.txt](https://theconversation.com/robots.txt)); Mongabay is CC BY-ND ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/copyright/creative-commons/)); Knowable is CC BY-ND and Carbon Brief non-commercial and unadapted, both from search snippets only; ProPublica is CC BY-NC-ND and forbids wholesale or automatic republication ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/steal-our-stories)). OpenStax's help centre now states CC BY-NC-SA ([OpenStax](https://help.openstax.org/s/article/Licensing-information-of-OpenStax-textbooks)), contradicting both the brief's premise and a Hugging Face copy labelled CC BY; sources conflict on whether some titles remain CC BY, and per-book licences were not checked. MIT OCW explicitly permits non-commercial AI training but binds derived models to the same terms. WikiHow is CC BY-NC-SA and under active enforcement, with a DMCA takedown on Hugging Face and a lawsuit against OpenAI in August 2026 ([HF discussion](https://huggingface.co/datasets/gursi26/wikihow-cleaned/discussions/1); [IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/08/25/wikihow-files-lawsuit-against-openai-over-chatgpts-use-of-its-how-to-library/)). Cochrane plain-language summaries run through publisher permissions, despite a CC BY label on one dataset card.

Ready-made datasets fail on provenance. ELI5 is defunct, its licence is "unknown" and Reddit's terms are hostile ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/defunct-datasets/eli5)); CNN/DailyMail, XSum and Multi-News are copyrighted news under mislabelled, unknown or non-commercial terms; the AI-detection sets (HC3, RAID, MAGE, M4) put permissive labels over mixed Reddit, BBC and WikiHow text, and going upstream is strictly better. Common Pile's own paper names the pattern licence laundering ([Common Pile](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05209)).

A last group is open but the wrong register. Wikipedia, Simple English Wikipedia and WikiText are CC BY-SA and encyclopedic, which is the register LLMs already default to, with the highest memorization and, for recent dumps, no way to filter out LLM-assisted edits. Pre-1930 science writers are public domain and a century old in diction; *Calculus Made Easy* is the closest fit and also heavily downloaded ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33283)). GAO highlights and BillSum are bureaucratic or legalistic; MedlinePlus is list-heavy and excludes its A.D.A.M. encyclopedia ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/about/using/usingcontent/)); VOA Learning English is public domain only where VOA-exclusive, uses a learner's vocabulary, and has an uncertain archive. PLOS author summaries are CC BY and large but short and measurably harder to read than eLife's. Science Journal for Kids is CC BY and pitched too young. NASA Earth Observatory is the liveliest agency prose by the researchers' background judgement, but NASA's public statement covers media, not contractor-written article text, and no sample could be read.

## Nothing is settled until these pages are re-read by hand

The research has four systematic limits. The fetch tool summarised pages and returned quotes as short fragments, truncating long pages at 100,000 characters, so even "verified" licence lines are fragments of a page nobody read in full. Many pages refused automated access, including Stack Exchange's licensing page, PLOS, Cochrane, Knowable, Khan Academy, nih.gov and congress.gov. No corpus was sampled at length, so every register ranking rests on descriptions, one or two pages, or prior familiarity. And word counts other than Wikinews, Gwern's 4.38 million and the Common Pile document counts are computed or estimated. The research papers carry the same caveat: figures came through a summarising tool, and items marked snippet-level above were not opened. None of the notes checked the licence of the base model itself.

The checks below come before any crawl or download. Reading pages and a handful of sample articles in a browser is the intended method.

| Creative Commons guidance | [CC guidance](https://creativecommons.org/using-cc-licensed-works-for-ai-training-2/) and the May 2025 legal primer | The ShareAlike and attribution passages in full; current status of CC signals |
| Alternates | [SSC About page](https://slatestarcodex.com/about/) footer and robots.txt; [gwern.net/about](https://gwern.net/about) | SSC licence wording and whether comments and guest posts are excluded; which Gwern paths hold third-party text |
| Register, by reading | Five to ten pieces from each shortlisted source | That the prose has the life the descriptions claim; the digest formula; prose share of Stack Exchange answers |
| Base model | Qwen3-8B licence | Terms for distributing an adapter; not covered by the notes |

Four consent requests are worth sending. Kalid Azad of Better Explained is the most valuable, because his corpus would close the mathematics gap and his licence already allows non-commercial use. A short note to Our World in Data would settle the "must not edit" line and ask whether a text export exists, which would also remove the crawl. A courtesy note to eLife would confirm digest coverage and the number of editors behind the voice. The ND publishers (The Conversation, Knowable, Mongabay) merit a request only if hand-read samples show their prose is clearly better than the shortlist; their licences already allow free republication and each lists a rights contact. Consent from a rights holder, which the project already has for its current author, remains the strongest basis for any single-publisher corpus.

## Conclusion

The search turned a question about which writer to imitate into a question about which editor. Every openly licensed single author found writes essays, and every well-licensed plain explainer is institutional; the middle ground is a small editorial team with a house style, and eLife's digest desk is the one instance with a clean licence, a pre-2022 archive and a ready dataset. That makes the first experiment cheap and informative whichever way it comes out: if an eLife-only adapter wins a blind test with frequent LLM users on science explainers, house style is enough and the blend is a coverage exercise; if it merely ties, the project has learned that "clean human register" is not a reason to prefer a text, and the single-voice options (OWID filtered to two authors, or a consented author) move up.

The licence picture is stable enough to plan on and not stable enough to rely on. The one decision that cannot be deferred is whether a published adapter may carry CC BY-SA, because it determines whether mathematics comes from Stack Exchange now or from Better Explained after a reply.

## Addendum, 2026-11-13: the target is textbook prose, and OpenStax is out

After this report the user set a different target: writing that sounds like a high-quality textbook (coherent, well-structured), judged by the user as an expert reader, with lay-reader preference rejected as a measure. That demotes digests, forum answers and news, and promotes open textbooks, which the report covered thinly. A follow-up licence check, book by book, is in `research_notes/informational-corpus/open_textbooks.md`. Its main findings, with the researcher's caveats:

- **OpenStax cannot be used.** All 19 prefaces opened state CC BY-NC-SA and add: "This book may not be used in the training of large language models or otherwise be ingested into large language models or generative AI offerings without OpenStax's prior written permission." OpenStax's licensing update is dated 22 April 2026. Older CC BY copies survive on LibreTexts; using them would go against the publisher's stated wishes, so they are excluded.
- **Usable under attribution-only or public-domain terms:** Peterson and Davie, *Computer Networks: A Systems Approach* (CC BY 4.0, source on GitHub; the book's website robots.txt disallows AI agents, so the repository is the only acceptable source); DeVoe, *Thermodynamics and Chemistry* (CC BY 4.0); Lane, *Online Statistics Education* (public domain); Erickson, *Algorithms* (CC BY 4.0, jokey register); *Open Data Structures* (CC BY 2.5 CA); Open Logic Project texts (CC BY 4.0, mostly formal notation); Austin and Kuttler on linear algebra (CC BY). Estimated 600,000 to 780,000 words for the first five; none measured.
- **Usable with ShareAlike:** Lebl's analysis and differential-equations books, Hefferon's *Linear Algebra*, OpenIntro Statistics, Crowell's physics series, SICP, Dive into Deep Learning, Ellingson's *Electromagnetics*. These fill mathematics and physics and take the total above an estimated 1.2 million words.
- **Private or non-commercial only:** MIT OpenCourseWare (its terms permit AI training, non-commercially, with derived models under the same licence), Downey's *Think* books, Trench.
- **Not usable:** How to Design Programs, Book of Proof, CORE Econ, Physically Based Rendering, Motion Mountain (NC-ND); Three Easy Pieces, Mathematics for Machine Learning, Tong's notes, the Feynman Lectures (all rights reserved).
- **Gaps:** no openly licensed life-science or economics textbook was verified. DeVoe, Kuttler and Earle licences were confirmed on catalogue pages, not the books' own copyright pages. Prose judgements rest on one section per book; Hefferon, OpenIntro and Crowell were not sampled.

**Baseline to beat.** Claude Opus, told in one sentence to write like a high-quality textbook, already defines terms, proceeds in steps and keeps one idea per paragraph on the four informational test briefs (`~/.quill/examples/claude-textbook-info.jsonl`). Its faults are stiffness: over-definition, short uniform sentences (spread 4.5 to 6.6 words), mechanical paragraph openers. A textbook-trained adapter has to beat that in the user's blind picks, not merely beat vanilla Claude.
